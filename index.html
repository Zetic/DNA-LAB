      How it works

      One shared template. Every species is drawn on the same fish template: the same body points and the same fin points, lined up by landmarks such as fin starts, fin ends and tail tips. Because the points match, any mix of species is just a weighted average of matching points.

      Shape genes. Each body region (body, dorsal fin, anal fin, tail, side fins) has one gene. A copy of that gene is a recipe like “60% clownfish, 40% betta”. A fish shows the average of its two copies, keeping only the four strongest species.

      Trait genes. Colors and patterns are on-or-off genes with dominant and recessive versions. A dominant copy hides a recessive one, so a fish can carry a trait it doesn’t show. Patterns are switched on as layers rather than averaged, so colors never turn muddy.

      Inheritance. For every gene, each parent passes on one of its two copies at random. That’s why siblings differ and hidden traits can come back. Crossing two pure species gives near-identical babies, because each one gets the same pair of copies; breed those babies with each other to see real variety. Both starting angelfish secretly carry albino, and the betta secretly carries spots.

      Mutation. Now and then a copy changes: a shape recipe drifts toward a random species, or a trait copy flips. Changed copies are marked “new” in the genome sheet.
